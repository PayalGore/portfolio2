Methodology

How I
Approach
Analytics

Most analysts start with the data. I start with the decision that needs to be made — then work backwards to figure out what data we actually need.

This sounds obvious. It rarely is. Most analytics projects fail not because the SQL was wrong, but because no one agreed on the question first.

Here are the three principles I apply to every project, from a five-minute ad-hoc query to a three-month pipeline build.

01 //
Question First,
SQL Second
I don't start with data. I start with: what decision are we making? Then I work backwards.

At IU Bloomington, leadership assumed low engagement was a motivation problem. I asked: when exactly are students logging in? The data showed an evening spike — but the peer-engagement features were buried after 6PM. That's a UI/UX problem, not a motivation problem. Saved the budget for the right fix.
02 //
Accuracy
Over Speed
Fast insights don't matter if they're wrong. Especially in financial reporting.

At DocGo, I built a three-layer validation model in Redshift. It took longer to build. But it reduced month-end close time by 30% because Finance stopped second-guessing the numbers.

Trust is compounding. One wrong dashboard destroys months of credibility.
03 //
Partner,
Don't Just Deliver
I sit with Finance VPs to understand ASC 606 compliance. I work with Product Managers to define KPIs before writing a single line of SQL. I review dashboards with end users before calling them done.

The SQL is just the implementation. The real work is understanding what question we're actually trying to answer — and making sure the output drives action, not just awareness.

Retention budget was heading to the wrong problem. Reframing the question saved significant spend and fixed the actual root cause.

The Question
Does protein bar vs. carbohydrate bar consumption affect post-meal calorie intake in the study cohort?
The Messy Data
250+ participants, manually collected nutrition logs, inconsistent meal timing records, outliers from non-compliance.
The Real Problem
Raw data had significant compliance gaps — participants who logged inconsistently skewed the intervention effect. Needed a cleaning and exclusion protocol before any statistical testing.
The Insight
Protein bars led to 20% fewer post-meal calories — but only after applying proper inclusion criteria and controlling for meal timing as a confounder.
The Decision
Study recommends protein bar intervention for the target population. Built CRM segmentation pipeline to identify the highest-impact participant profile for future campaigns.
The Outcome
25% boost in campaign participation using the targeted segment vs. broad outreach.
Why It Mattered
Public health interventions have real-world budget constraints. Wrong segmentation = wasted reach. The pipeline made targeting defensible to funders.
Key output
The lesson
"Cleaning the data isn't unglamorous — it's where the actual insight lives."

The Question
Why is spoilage running at 20% weekly and which categories are driving it?
The Messy Data
15+ produce categories, handwritten daily sales logs, seasonal demand inconsistency, supplier lead times that varied without notice.
The Real Problem
Procurement was based on intuition and habit, not demand signal. Certain categories (leafy greens, stone fruits) were being ordered at flat weekly volumes despite clear seasonal demand curves.
The Insight
3 categories drove 80% of spoilage. All three had predictable demand dips on specific days and after specific weather patterns.
The Decision
Built demand forecasting tracker in Excel with rolling 4-week averages and supplier cost benchmarking. Adjusted order quantities category by category.
The Outcome
20% reduction in weekly spoilage. Improved procurement margins and reduced cash tied up in inventory that didn't sell.
Why It Mattered
This is where I learned that data doesn't have to be "big" to be valuable. A spreadsheet built on the right question saved real money in a business with no safety net.

dbt
Moving Beyond SQL Scripts into Proper Data Transformation
Why: Finance teams need reproducible models, not one-off queries. A dbt pipeline with tests, documentation, and lineage is the difference between a report and a system. At DocGo, I saw firsthand how an undocumented SQL script becomes a liability the moment the person who wrote it leaves.
Semantic Layer Design
Bridging Raw Data and Business Metrics
Why: Reading Fundamentals of Data Engineering because the gap between raw data and the metric a Finance VP trusts is where most analytics projects fail. The semantic layer is where accuracy lives — and most teams skip it. I'm building the skill to design it right.
AI for Data Validation

Why: Experimenting with AI agents to flag anomalies in AR data before they reach the dashboard. Not replacing analysts — augmenting the validation layer. The goal is catching the edge case that slips through rule-based checks at 11PM on month-end close.
